If you have just read something alarming about AI and want to do something with that feeling, the useful next step is small and specific. Not "raise awareness". One message, to one body that has the authority to act on the thing you are worried about. This page exists to tell you which body that is.

Aim at the seat, not at your own representative

Almost every tool that helps people contact government about AI asks for your postcode and routes you to whoever represents you. That is the right instrument for some things and the wrong one here, because your own representative almost certainly has no jurisdiction over AI.

Legislatures divide work by committee. A handful of committees have AI explicitly in their remit, and within those, a small number of seats decide what gets heard: the chair, who controls whether a bill gets a hearing at all; the ranking or minority member, who can force attention from the other side; and in the European Parliament and Brazil, the rapporteur — the single member who drafts the text, and the most consequential seat in the whole process.

A message to the chair of the committee that hears AI bills carries more weight than the same message to your own member, even though you cannot vote for them. Many of these bodies accept submissions from anyone, and several accept them from outside the country entirely.

The exception worth knowing: if you want something to be counted rather than read — a constituent tally on a live vote — then your own representative is exactly right, and the existing tools listed further down do that job well.

What will go stale, and how fast

Named people churn. Chairs change with elections, reshuffles and committee reconstitutions; EU rapporteurs change with every file. Every named holder here carries the date it was checked. When a name looks wrong, follow the membership link — that page is maintained by the institution and will be right. The structure of who holds jurisdiction changes far more slowly than who occupies the seat, and that structure is the part of this page worth trusting.

One example of how fast this moves: the UK's Department for Science, Innovation and Technology was abolished in July 2026, and the Commons committee that scrutinises this area reverted to its older name. Anything written before that is now partly wrong — including, in places, the companion page to this one.
